# Shared attribution rules and decision cards

## Attribution scope

The scenario can give depicted conduct its contextual meaning, including the alternatives involved, what a response concerns, or the purpose or time horizon of an arrangement. These meanings need not be independently identifiable from the image alone. Use ordinary contextual inference to understand how the depiction realizes the situation. When the scenario leaves a choice or response open, it does not establish which alternative or stance the image expresses; judge that from the depiction interpreted in context. If the image contradicts the scenario, follow the image; if the two together leave an endpoint unsupported, do not mark it as supported.

Interpret each observation or possible depiction through its role in the focal situation. Establish what it contributes to understanding the particular matter, such as what a participant is responding to, how alternatives are treated, or how a relationship or arrangement is organized. An element may be associated with an endpoint in isolation while providing context for a different orientation in this situation. Its presence or relevance to the matter alone does not establish endpoint support. Do not substitute a different purpose, benefit, or contrast, or introduce a separate annotation target merely to retain an endpoint interpretation associated with one element.

For example, in a scenario about a customer's reaction to preferential treatment in a store, a depicted VIP-only area may establish what the customer is reacting to. Its presence alone does not establish high PDI in the focal situation: the same arrangement could be something the customer accepts or challenges. Even if a scenario-derived scene or an image candidate links the VIP area to high PDI, verify how the customer's depicted response, understood in the scenario's context, treats the unequal standing involved before accepting that interpretation.

Consider overall presentation, including atmosphere and composition, as well as participants, objects, institutions, text, spatial arrangements, and background details. Interpret their roles from the scenario and surrounding depiction; neither visual prominence nor the category of an element determines whether it can contribute evidence. The image may introduce meanings not specified by the scenario; include them when they contribute to the value orientation expressed through the focal situation. Apply all six dimensions within this same scope.

## Shared Evidence Standard

Assess evidence by connecting what is depicted, the role and meaning of that depiction in the focal situation, and the endpoint support provided by that role under its decision card. Make this connection explicit in the interpretation. Consider relevant cues together: a detail that helps establish what a response concerns may contribute to interpreting that response without independently supporting the endpoint associated with the detail. A candidate need not explain the whole image by itself, but its proposed contribution must remain warranted when considered with the surrounding depiction. Explain the orientation expressed in this situation, not merely a familiar benefit, motive, or value associated with the activity in general.

- Sufficient support: Explain how the depiction, interpreted in the scenario's context, meets the endpoint criteria rather than merely naming its activity, setting, or topic. You need not prove private motives, exclude every conceivable alternative, or find explicit conflict or a depicted opposite endpoint.
- Positive evidence: Support an endpoint through what the depiction expresses, not merely through the absence of something associated with its opposite.
- Visible text: Readable text may contribute when it bears on the focal situation. Interpret it together with the surrounding depiction; a topic word alone does not establish an endpoint. Do not guess unreadable text, and do not require text when other visual evidence is sufficient.
- Multiple dimensions: The same cues may support more than one dimension; separate visual cues are not required. For each supported endpoint, explain how the depiction meets that dimension's particular criteria; a justification for one dimension does not automatically justify another.
- Cultural and demographic cues: Do not assign values based on presumed national, ethnic, gender, or other group characteristics. Appearance and setting may help interpret the depicted situation, but group identity or a cultural marker alone is not endpoint evidence.

## Scenario-Specific Guidance

The following guidance covers some common scenario forms, not every possible situation. Use any that help interpret the image in context; one, several, or none may apply. Regardless, assess all six dimensions using the rubrics below.

### Choice or follow-through

Use the scenario to understand the alternatives and whether the target is a choice, commitment, or its implementation; a choice need not already be carried out. When the scenario establishes a meaningful contrast between alternatives, the image may support an endpoint in either of two ways:

- **Multiple alternatives are depicted, with a clear choice:** Interpret the indicated choice in the scenario’s context and assess its meaning against the endpoint criteria.
- **One alternative is clearly realized:** The depiction, understood in the scenario’s context, may establish that realization without showing the act of choosing or the other alternatives.

Merely showing available alternatives does not establish a choice, and an object associated with an alternative does not by itself establish its realization. Once the choice or realization is clear, no additional choice gesture or depiction of the opposing option is required. Interpret each alternative through its role in the depicted choice or realization: an unchosen option may clarify what the choice means, but its presence does not create a second endpoint. Do not mark endpoints that depend on the contrast as supported when the relevant choice or realization remains unresolved.

### Reaction, response, acceptance, rejection, or adoption

Use the scenario to interpret what a response is directed toward, then examine how the depiction supports the participant's stance. A response may be expressed through the combined depiction, including expression, posture, spatial relationships, and composition, not only an overt action. Interpret the practice or arrangement being responded to in that relation: it may establish the reason for, object of, or contrast needed to understand the response. Its presence neither establishes the respondent's orientation nor supplies a separate endpoint merely because that practice or arrangement is associated with one. An additional endpoint needs its own evidential connection within the focal situation, beyond separately labeling the response's object. A system's operation alone does not establish a participant's adoption, though their use and response may support it. If only the prompting situation is shown, do not infer the missing response.

### Relationship or arrangement

When an ongoing relationship or arrangement is itself the focal matter, evaluate how it is depicted, rather than requiring a discrete choice or final outcome. Consider how participants, objects, resources, and space are organized and presented. The arrangement may directly provide evidence without a separate act of endorsement, submission, or protest; the decision cards specify the endpoint connection it must establish. When an arrangement instead provides context for another focal matter, assess its contribution through that role under the shared attribution scope.

## Six Fixed Decision Cards

Each card defines what to assess, how its endpoints differ, when evidence is sufficient, and common confusions. Apply every card within the shared attribution scope above: its evidence routes explain how an endpoint can be expressed, but do not make every depicted instance an independent annotation target. The routes are not exhaustive checklists of required objects.

Use the first step of each card to determine whether its defining issue is involved in the depicted focal situation, understood in the scenario’s context. If so, identify the specific matter or relationship being assessed; do not assume that one must be found for every dimension. If no relevant matter can be identified, neither endpoint is established. Otherwise, use the second step to assess what orientation, if any, the depiction supports. A relevant matter can be present without sufficient support for either endpoint.

Consider the focal situation as a whole, including relevant visual content and presentation. The scenario may help establish what depicted elements mean, and the image may introduce a relevant aspect that the scenario did not specify. An activity or object may establish relevance through its meaning in context, but a topical association or an imagined extension of the situation cannot. The same cues may support both applicability and an endpoint; separate cues, an explicit conflict, or two depicted alternatives are not required.
